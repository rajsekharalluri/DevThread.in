# Conflict Resolution in Engineering Teams

## Introduction
Conflict can be technical, process/ownership-related, or interpersonal. The first leadership skill is diagnosing which problem is actually present; the resolution approach differs for each.

```text
Visible argument
      ↓ ask “what is really blocked?”
Technical disagreement? Process gap? Interpersonal behavior?
Decision/process change or direct conversation
Follow-up and prevention
```

## Purpose
Healthy disagreement improves decisions. Unresolved or personal conflict causes people to stop raising concerns, avoid collaboration, or route work around each other, which damages both delivery and team health.

## Real-world simple example
Two engineers repeatedly argue about a state-management approach. The apparent conflict may be a missing team standard rather than personality friction:

```text
No agreed architectural rule
Repeated review disagreement
Different expectations
Personal frustration
```

Facilitate a decision on criteria/standard and document it instead of telling both people merely to “get along.”

## Professional company-level approach
For interpersonal behavior, speak privately and specifically:

```text
Observation: “In yesterday's review, you interrupted three times.”
Impact: “The author stopped explaining the risk.”
Expectation: “Let people finish and challenge the proposal, not the person.”
Follow-up: “We will check the next reviews together.”
```

Do not label character (“you are disrespectful”); describe observable behavior and impact.

## Common failure scenarios
- Avoiding the conversation until the issue becomes a crisis.
- Taking sides before hearing both perspectives.
- Treating process ambiguity as personality conflict.
- Letting a technical debate continue without a decision owner.
- Publicly correcting or humiliating someone.

## Comparison
| Conflict type | Useful response |
|---|---|
| Technical | Criteria, evidence, decision owner |
| Ownership/process | Clarify responsibility/authority |
| Interpersonal | Private behavior-impact conversation |
| Performance | Specific feedback and support plan |
| Safety/harassment | Immediate formal escalation |

## Interview Questions
- **[L1]** What types of conflict appear in engineering teams?
- **[L1]** Why is avoiding conflict usually harmful?
- **[L2]** How do you handle a disagreement affecting team dynamics?
- **[L2]** How do you distinguish healthy debate from harmful conflict?
- **[L3]** How would you resolve a repeated conflict between senior engineers?
- **[L3]** What should happen when behavior crosses into harassment or safety risk?

## Interview Answers
1. Technical disagreement, process/ownership conflict, interpersonal friction, and performance/safety issues are common categories.
2. Avoidance allows assumptions and resentment to grow; people often disengage instead of raising useful concerns.
3. Hear perspectives privately, identify root cause, choose a technical/process/direct-conversation response, make a decision, and follow up.
4. Healthy debate focuses on the problem and converges; harmful conflict becomes personal, repeats without resolution, or suppresses participation.
5. Establish decision criteria/owner for technical issues, address behavior privately with specific evidence, and set follow-up expectations rather than forcing artificial consensus.
6. Follow company policy and formal escalation immediately. Safety/harassment is not a normal debate to mediate informally.

## Expert perspective
The goal is not zero disagreement. The goal is a team where disagreement remains evidence-based, converges to a decision, and people feel safe raising concerns without personal retaliation.
