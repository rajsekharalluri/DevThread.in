# Mentoring and Growing Engineers

## Introduction
Mentoring is deliberately improving another engineer's capability and judgment over time. It is not just answering the immediate question or doing the work faster yourself.

```text
Goal/skill gap
Slightly stretching opportunity
Guiding questions + safe support
Practice/feedback
Independent capability
```

## Purpose
A team's long-term capability grows when people receive context, practice, feedback, and increasing ownership. Mentoring reduces single points of failure and helps engineers grow into broader responsibilities.

## Real-world simple example
Instead of immediately fixing a junior engineer's bug:

```text
“What does the error say?”
“What assumption might be wrong?”
“What would you inspect first?”
```

The mentor helps the person learn a repeatable debugging process rather than providing a one-time patch they cannot explain.

## Professional company-level example
A growth plan connects a person's goal to observable opportunities:

```text
Goal: grow toward senior ownership
Evidence opportunities:
  ├── lead a design review
  ├── own a production change
  ├── mentor another engineer
  ├── write a migration/rollback plan
  └── communicate risk to stakeholders
```

The mentor provides context, reviews progress, and gradually removes support. The engineer owns the work and evidence; mentoring is not a hidden promotion promise.

## Useful mentoring behaviors

- Ask before telling.
- Explain reasoning, not only the answer.
- Give safe opportunities slightly beyond current comfort.
- Give feedback near the event, not months later.
- Include people who are less visible or less similar to you.
- Let a learner struggle safely instead of rescuing immediately.
- Celebrate improved judgment, not only successful output.

## Common failure scenarios
- Mentor becomes a permanent dependency.
- Only high-visibility people receive opportunities.
- Feedback is vague: “show more ownership.”
- Mentoring becomes unpaid task completion for the mentee.
- The mentor assumes one career path fits everyone.

## Comparison
| Activity | Main purpose |
|---|---|
| Mentoring | Long-term growth and judgment |
| Coaching | Improve a specific skill/behavior |
| Teaching | Transfer structured knowledge |
| Pairing | Solve/work while demonstrating practice |
| Managing | Accountability, priorities, performance/support |

## Interview Questions
- **[L1]** What is mentoring?
- **[L1]** Why should a mentor ask questions instead of always giving answers?
- **[L2]** How do you mentor with limited time?
- **[L2]** How do you choose a safe growth opportunity?
- **[L3]** How do you avoid creating dependency on the mentor?
- **[L3]** How do you make mentoring fair across a diverse team?

## Interview Answers
1. Mentoring is sustained support that builds another engineer's skills, judgment, confidence, and ability to operate independently.
2. Questions make the learner reason through the problem and build a reusable mental model instead of memorizing one solution.
3. Embed it in normal work: explanatory review comments, focused pairing, regular short check-ins, and deliberate delegation.
4. Choose a task slightly beyond current skill with bounded risk, clear success criteria, checkpoints, and a safe rollback/support path.
5. Give ownership to the learner, use questions, reduce help over time, rotate opportunities, and measure whether they solve similar future problems independently.
6. Use transparent growth expectations, rotate high-impact opportunities, avoid selecting only people who resemble you, and document evidence rather than relying on visibility or affinity.

## Expert perspective
Mentoring is successful when the mentee no longer needs the mentor for the same class of problem. The short-term cost of letting someone learn is an investment in a team that can operate without a single expert bottleneck.
